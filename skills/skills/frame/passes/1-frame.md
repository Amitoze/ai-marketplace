# Pass 1 — Frame

**Goal:** turn the raw statement into ONE extracted problem with the
frame deliberately chosen, not inherited. Not the "real" problem — a
*better* problem to solve.

**Sits on:** Wedell-Wedellsborg, Frame → Reframe → Move Forward (HBR
Jan–Feb 2017, "Are You Solving the Right Problems?"; *What's Your
Problem?*, HBR Press 2020) · Hayakawa's abstraction ladder (*Language in
Thought and Action*, 1939) · Ackoff's messes-vs-problems distinction.

## Output (dossier §1)

- The problem exactly as first stated, verbatim — the frame hides in
  the wording, so never paraphrase it before writing it down.
- The abstraction ladder: the mess above, concrete manifestations below.
- 2–3 rival frames, each with the reason it was rejected. The rejects
  are the proof reframing happened; a §1 with no rejects is incomplete.
- The kept frame.
- Sibling problems extracted from the mess, parked as named future
  dossiers.

## Process

1. **Write it down verbatim.** Ask the user to state the problem; record
   it untouched.
2. **Ladder it.** Up: "why does this matter?" until you reach the mess —
   the system of interrelated problems this one was pulled from (Ackoff:
   reality serves up messes; problems are things we *extract*). Down:
   "how does it show up, concretely?" until you reach observable
   incidents. Most bad statements are two rungs welded together — the
   ladder un-welds them.
3. **Attack with the five reframing strategies** (the question bank
   below). Generate rival frames before accepting the user's.
4. **Extract ONE problem.** The kept frame at one rung. Siblings get a
   name and a parking note, not a share of this dossier.
5. **Move forward.** If a cheap real-world test of the kept frame exists
   (one conversation, one observation), name it — frames are validated
   by reality, not by debate in the room.

## Question bank — the five strategies

1. *Look outside the frame:* who or what does the statement omit? Who
   else touches this problem?
2. *Rethink the goal:* what is the goal behind the stated goal? (Slow
   elevator → happier tenants: mirrors, not motors.)
3. *Examine bright spots:* when does the problem NOT occur? What is
   different about those times?
4. *Look in the mirror:* what is the user's own contribution to the
   problem as stated?
5. *Take their perspective:* how would the other party in the problem
   state it? (Often the highest-yield question — it surfaces frames the
   first-person view cannot see.)

## Exit check

§1 holds a verbatim statement, a ladder, ≥2 recorded rejects with
reasons, one kept frame at one rung, and parked siblings. If a solution
already exists for this problem, the kept frame has survived the
solution-in-hand check from the conduct rules.
